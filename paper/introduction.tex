\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Submarine telecommunications cables carry most intercontinental data traffic \todo{\cite{chesnoy2016undersea}}, and their inspection, burial assessment, repair and protection all start with knowing where they are.
Because the cable is often buried, acoustic and optical methods lose it, and electromagnetic sensing becomes the main modality that penetrates the sediments.
These cables carry a DC current of about \SI{1}{\ampere} to power the repeaters along the way, with the sea as the return path, and they therefore produce a static line-current field.
At a distance of \SIrange{2}{4}{\metre} from the cable this field lies between \SI{50}{\nano\tesla} and \SI{100}{\nano\tesla}, three orders of magnitude below the geomagnetic field of about \SI{48000}{\nano\tesla} \todo{\cite{alken2021igrf}}.

In previous work we derived two orthonormal basis functions, called cable OBF, matched to an infinite line current \todo{\cite{hleis2026obf}}.
The resulting generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT) detects the cable with fewer noise dimensions and places the closest point of approach (CPA) more accurately than dipole and multipolar receivers, both in simulation and in a lake experiment.

Detection alone does not follow a cable.
A tracker must turn each detection into a description of the cable, fuse these descriptions over successive passes, and decide where the next pass goes, all under the motion and calibration errors of a small submarine drone \todo{\cite{xiang2016subsea,yu2018robust,zhang2024electromagnetic}}.

This paper does not present the full autonomous tracking loop and focuses instead on the observation stage.
We translate the filter output of the first paper into observations of the cable that do not depend on the length of the pass, namely its location, bearing and standoff.
By working in the latent space spanned by the cable OBF rather than on raw field measurements, where the data are less noisy and more consistent, the paper bridges two research communities.
The first is geometry-based cable tracking in robotics, and the second is signal processing for low signal-to-noise ratio.
Methods from robotics can thus operate on the latent space of the cable OBF.

The contributions of this paper are the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an amplitude and phase description of every OBF channel,
  \item a localization whose noiseless displacement under truncation is zero,
  \item closed-form expressions that extend the observation to bearing and standoff.
\end{itemize}

We assume a submarine drone moving at constant depth and velocity that follows the cable by repeatedly crossing it in a zigzag pattern.
